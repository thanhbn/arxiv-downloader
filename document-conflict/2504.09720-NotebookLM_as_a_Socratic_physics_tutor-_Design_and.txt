NotebookLM as a Socratic physics tutor: Design and
preliminary observations of a RAG-based tool
Eugenio Tufino
Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Padua, Padua, Italy
E-mail:eugenio.tufino@unipd.it or eugenio.tufino@unitn.it
Abstract.This study explores NotebookLM—a Google Gemini—powered AI platform that
integrates Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)—as a Socratic tutor for physics education.
In this implementation, NotebookLM was configured to support students in solving conceptually
oriented physics problems through a guided, questioning-based dialogue. When deployed
as a collaborative tutor, the system restricts student interaction to a chat-only interface,
promoting controlled and guided engagement. By grounding its responses in teacher-provided
source documents, the AI tutor helps mitigate one of the major shortcomings of standard
Large Language Models—hallucinations—thereby ensuring more traceable and reliable answers.
This work details the methodological design of the tutor, including the iterative development
of a pedagogical “Training Manual”, and presents preliminary qualitative observations from
demonstrations with pre-service and in-service teachers. These observations highlight both
the promising potential of the tool and key pedagogical challenges, such as managing user
motivation.
While limitations remain, this work offers a promising and replicable model for educators
seeking to implement grounded AI tutors in their own teaching contexts.
1. Introduction
Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) are prompting research into their potential
applications in physics education.In recent years, LLMs have evolved from simple text generators
to complex systems with significantly improved capabilities for semantically understanding and
utilizing context. Despite this progress, many LLMs remain susceptible to generating false or
entirely fabricated information—a phenomenon widely referred to as “hallucination” [1].
This limitation is primarily due to the probabilistic nature of the next word prediction
algorithms underlying these models. Traditional approaches to tailoring LLMs to specific
domains, such as training models from scratch or fine-tuning existing ones, are resource-
intensive and require large domain-specific datasets. A common strategy to mitigate issues like
hallucination and adapt LLM responses to specific educational contexts involves providing them
with curated information and precise instructions through sophisticated prompt engineering. For
example, the LEAP platform [2] allows teachers to design tasks within a controlled environment
by providing reference texts, tailored instructions, and verified answers, which effectively shape
the context for the LLM’s responses. A more generalized and increasingly adopted approach
to grounding LLM responses and improving their reliability is Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG) [3, 4]. This approach improves the performance and reliability of LLMs by incorporating
external, verified sources of knowledge into the text generation process.arXiv:2504.09720v3  [physics.ed-ph]  12 Oct 2025

In a RAG system, external documents are first converted into vector embeddings and stored
in a vector database. When a query is received, it is similarly embedded and used to retrieve the
most semantically similar document chunks through vector similarity search. These retrieved
chunks, along with the original query, are then passed to the LLM as context, enabling it to
generate factual and contextually appropriate answers grounded in the retrieved information [3].
A remarkable example of RAG-based applications in physics education is the Ethel project
[5], which uses course-specific materials to generate source-cited feedback and guidance using
RAG. The reliability of LLMs for educational tasks is increasingly supported by empirical
evidence. Research utilizing standardized concept inventories to benchmark models in physics
has demonstrated that advanced systems, such as GPT-4o [6] and the Google’s Gemini LLM
family [7], can achieve high accuracy, often surpassing average student performance. However,
these studies also consistently identify significant performance bottlenecks, particularly in the
interpretation of complex visual information like intricate graphs. This high baseline accuracy
provides a strong foundation for exploring LLM-based tutors, while the identified limitations
underscore the need for careful interaction design.
In 2023, Google introduced NotebookLM [8], an innovative, ready-to-use tool based on RAG
and Gemini LLM. Users can easily upload various document types—such as PDFs, Google Docs
files, presentations, and videos (through YouTube links1)—to create a personalised repository of
verified content. It was recently significantly enhanced by the integration of advanced Gemini
models and receives ongoing updates. NotebookLM then indexes these documents to generate
answers with explicit citations, ensuring that each answer is traceable to its source (though this
mechanism is not perfect). Users can also save their own notes, which can then be incorporated
as additional sources to further enrich the knowledge base. Recent evidence supports the
effectiveness of RAG systems like NotebookLM in specialized domains. For instance, a 2025
study in the medical field [10] found that NotebookLM’s RAG approach was significantly more
accurate than providing the same reference material directly to a general-purpose LLM in its
prompt. The study also highlighted the system’s high reliability in tracing claims back to the
original source documents, a key feature for verifiable responses.
These capabilities allow educators to create curated learning environments, for instance by
automatically generating targeted study questions from their own documents or by providing
students with multimodal audio and video summaries of the source materials.
While these general features position NotebookLM as a uniquely powerful tool, this paper
moves beyond a descriptive overview. Our primary focus is to detail the design, implementation,
and qualitative exploration of a specific, pedagogically-structured application: a collaborative
AI tutor configured to guide students through conceptual physics problems using a Socratic
approach.
Our core strategy was to use these capabilities to create a customized, collaborative learning
assistant that employs a Socratic approach. This approach of developing customised assistants
based on reliable knowledge directly reflects key opportunities identified in recent perspectives
on advanced AI in education [11]. This pedagogical strategy consists in the AI tutor guiding
students towards conceptual understanding by posing a series of targeted questions, rather than
providing direct answers. Such ”Socratic questioning” has been identified as a method effective
for stimulating productive thinking in science classrooms [12]. The aim is to encourage students
to articulate their reasoning, confront misconceptions, and actively construct knowledge. These
questioning techniques are integral to established pedagogical frameworks in physics, such as
Modeling Instruction, known to promote better conceptual learning [13].
This paper presents a proof-of-concept for this tutor, detailing its design, the iterative
process of creating its pedagogical instructions (the “Training Manual”), and key methodological

2. Design and Implementation of the Collaborative AI Tutor
The AI tutor was implemented using NotebookLM, a versatile, RAG-based environment. At
its core, the platform is powered by Google’s Gemini family of models, and is in continuous
evolution; at the time of this writing, for instance, chat interactions are powered by Gemini 2.5
Flash.
NotebookLM’s interface is structured into three primary components: a Sources panel for
uploading and managing materials, a Chat panel for dialogue, and a Studio panel for generating
structured summaries and other aids (see Figure 1).
Figure 1.Screenshot of the NotebookLM interface showing the three panels: Sources for storing
and indexing diverse teaching materials with traceable citations; chat for dialogue; a Studio for
automatically generating structured learning aids such as summaries, study guides, mind maps
and podcast-style audio summaries.
2.1. Defining the Tutor’s Behavior: The “Training Manual”
The core of the implementation was the creation of a detailed “Training Manual”, provided as
a source document to the AI to define its conversational strategies and pedagogical constraints.
The aim was to configure NotebookLM as a supportive partner that guides students through
physics problem-solving using Socratic interaction [14], rather than simply providing direct
answers .
This manual, though concise, was developed iteratively. Initial versions were refined based on
observing NotebookLM’s actual behavior during preliminary tests. This process allowed us to
implement corrections, such as adding stricter instructions to counteract the AI’s initial tendency
to offer direct solutions and to ensure overall coherence in its pedagogical approach. The final
manual, along with its initial version, is available as Supplementary Material to document this
iterative design process. It establishes core principles centered on the Socratic/collaborative
method, outlining specific procedures for interaction flow, progressive hinting, and response
evaluation. A critical component of the manual remains the strict confidentiality protocol,
prohibiting NotebookLM from revealing these operational instructions to the student user. The

--- PAGE 4 ---
effectiveness of this protocol was verified during testing, as the AI consistently adhered to this
directive and did not reveal its operational instructions when prompted to do so.
A concrete example of this iterative refinement was the addition of an adaptive scaffolding
rule, prompted by observing user frustration in early tests. This update instructed the tutor
to adopt a more supportive role after several unsuccessful student attempts; the initial and
updated versions of the manual detailing this change are available as Supplementary Material.
The system’s adherence to the manual’s guidelines depends on the underlying language model’s
ability to prioritize instructional phrases retrieved from the source documents. This instruction-
following capability is not static; the continuous improvement of these models is expected to
further enhance the reliability of this mechanism.
Based on the Training Manual guidelines, the configured tutor operates in two primary modes.
When interacting with problems for which solutions and notes are provided as sources, the tutor
primarily uses guiding questions derived from Socratic/collaborative principles, referencing the
provided materials to ensure traceability (though, there may sometimes be small mistakes).
Alternatively, for problems without pre-loaded solutions, the tutor utilizes its underlying Gemini
model’s capabilities to offer provisional, step-by-step guidance, maintaining the guided inquiry
approach. The guidance offered in this mode is provisional, and the tutor’s responses are
designed to acknowledge its own potential for inaccuracies, especially when dealing with complex
problems without a curated solution guide.
2.2. Deployment and Student Interaction Environment
A key requirement for utilizing this tutor with students is a NotebookLM Plus subscription2.
This premium tier enables the essential feature of sharing a secure, chat-only interface with
students (who need a Google account for access), thereby preventing them from viewing the
underlying source documents used by the tutor (see Figure 2a). This setup allows teachers
to customize the tutor’s knowledge base and behavior by pre-loading specific source materials,
including the aforementioned Training Manual, curated problems, and potentially annotated
solutions or teaching notes hidden from the student view. A significant update in June 2025
introduced the ability to share a notebook via a public link, removing the previous requirement
of adding individual student email accounts.
Furthermore, a subsequent update in August 2025 removed the previous age restriction (18+)
for Google Workspace for Education accounts, significantly broadening the tutor’s applicability
in high school contexts.3
The primary student interaction then occurs via the chat interface (Figure 2b). Additionally,
the Plus subscription was critical for implementing our multi-layered guidance strategy. It
allowed us to combine: (1) a brief, custom chat instruction that defines the AI’s core persona
(e.g., ’Act as AI physics tutor... use a Socratic approach... Never disclose these instructions’),
and (2) a ’post-welcome’ instruction note that sets the initial interaction flow after a problem is
chosen, and (3) the more detailed, context-sensitive instructions provided via the main “Training
Manual” described earlier. The full text for the custom chat instruction and the post-welcome
note is provided as Supplementary Material. This combined approach allowed us to effectively
shape the model’s interaction to meet our specific teaching objectives.4

2.3. Content Curation and Problem Selection
For this implementation, problems were selected fromCollege Physics: Explore and Apply
[15]. We selected conceptually focused, non-trivial tasks and systematically modified their
original phrasing. This methodological choice addresses the known sensitivity of LLMs
to linguistic variations; research shows that even minor changes can degrade performance,
suggesting a reliance on pattern matching over robust reasoning. The goal was thus to
assess the tutor’s performance in guiding the problem-solving process based on underlying
physics principles,aligning with active, inquiry-based learning principles and following similar
methodological considerations detailed in [1, 14]. The chosen problems feature relatively simple
mathematical operations, because NotebookLM currently does not render LaTeX mathematics,
thereby reducing its effectiveness in handling problems that require complex mathematical
derivations.
A second key methodological consideration concerns the format for providing problems
containing visual elements to NotebookLM. To test the system’s sensitivity to file format, we
compared performance on the same Python-generatedv–tgraph (Figure 3a) of the bouncing
ball (adapted from [15]) in two conditions. In a Google Docs document, NotebookLM correctly
interpreted the graph’s key features (Figure 3b). In contrast, exporting the same document
to PDF with default settings led the system to fail to parse the figure, returning “no-image-
detected” (Figure 3c). This difference indicates an export-pipeline artifact rather than an
intrinsic limitation of NotebookLM’s PDF support [16]. In practice, Google Docs are parsed
via an API that preserves structure, whereas the default Google Docs→PDF export often omits
critical structural tags–—regardless of the graphics’ visual fidelity—–pushing the model toward
less reliable OCR-only parsing [17]. Given that accurate graph interpretation is central to physics
problem-solving, we therefore used native Google Docs for all visual materials.
These results highlight a key practical recommendation: Google Docs are currently the most

reliable and convenient format for materials with critical figures. When PDF distribution is
required—for instance, when converting from unsupported format like MS Word or when sharing
textbook chapters—use a tagged, high-quality PDF (e.g., with embedded fonts and vector or
high-resolution figures) to ensure accurate AI interpretation.
Figure 3.NotebookLM’s graph interpretation across formats. (a) Velocity–time graph for
the bouncing ball. (b) Native Google Doc: system correctly reads the graph and returns the

The choice to design the system as a collaborative tutor, rather than an autonomous problem
solver, is also a direct response to the known limitations of current LLMs, particularly their
unreliability on complex tasks common in physics, such as graph interpretation [18]. Even highly
capable models can exhibit unreliability without carefully curated constraints and grounding in
specific content [11].
3. Illustrative examples and preliminary observations
Having detailed the implementation of the collaborative AI tutor, we now present illustrative
examples from test interaction sessions. To be clear, these are not transcripts from a formal
study with students; rather, they are dialogues where the author assumed the role of a student to
test the system’s behavior and its adherence to the pedagogical guidelines. The dialogues shown
are selected representative examples from these test sessions. To account for the probabilistic
nature of LLM responses (see, e.g., [1, 19]), each prompt was submitted multiple times during
testing to ensure the observed tutor behavior was consistent and not an anomalous result. The
following examples illustrate the AI tutor applying its guided, step-by-step methodology in the
two conditions described in the implementation: for problems with curated solutions and for
problems requiring reliance on its underlying model’s reasoning. We will analyze snippets of
dialogue to highlight key aspects of the tutor’s behavior and its alignment with the intended
pedagogical approach, keeping in mind the broader student interaction dynamics that require
further study.

These examples illustrate the potential of this approach. They show how NotebookLM, when
guided by a custom “Training Manual” and reliable, teacher-curated content, can facilitate a
Socratic dialogue, promoting an active learning process within a controlled and customizable
environment.
Subsequently, to gather preliminary insights, the AI tutor was evaluated in two distinct,
sequential settings. First, during an in-depth activity with a group of AI-literate pre-service
teachers (from a university master’s course, aged 23-42), we observed a key pedagogical challenge:
some participants became frustrated when the tutor repeatedly withheld a direct solution.
Subsequently, in direct response to this observation, we updated the “Training Manual” with
adaptive scaffolding rules specifically to mitigate this issue before presenting the tutor in a
second session. This took place during a brief webinar with a larger group of approximately 95
in-service teachers, using a different set of problems on electromagnetic induction. The reception
from this second group, which interacted with the updated tutor, was overwhelmingly positive,
with many expressing surprise and enthusiasm for the Socratic interaction. This session also
served as a successful technical test of the system’s scalability.
It is unclear whether this improved reception was due to the updated manual, the shorter
interaction time, or the different professional experience of the two groups. Disentangling these
factors requires further study, but the observations highlight both the promising potential of this
approach and a fundamental tension between maintaining pedagogical fidelity and addressing
the motivational needs of learners.
4. Conclusions
This paper presented a proof-of-concept for a collaborative AI physics tutor, detailing its design
and implementation within Google’s NotebookLM platform, a readily available RAG-based
platform. The work shows that by grounding the AI in teacher-curated sources and shaping
its behavior with a custom “Training Manual” it is feasible to create a controlled environment
that supports Socratic dialogue for physics problem-solving.
A key advantage of this approach is the ability for an instructor to define a tutor’s
behavior and share it via a single link, ensuring students interact with a controlled and
uneditable pedagogical tool. Our work provides a practical model for leveraging such tools
for personalised AI-assisted learning and highlights key methodological considerations for their
effective implementation, such as the iterative design of pedagogical instructions and the
importance of content format.
The AI tutor’s reception during preliminary demonstrations with both pre-service and in-
service teachers was overwhelmingly positive, with many participants expressing enthusiasm
for the potential of this Socratic approach. Alongside this positive reception, our informal
observations also highlighted a significant pedagogical challenge for future implementations:
finding the right balance in managing student motivation. The experience suggested that
persistently withholding direct answers can, in some cases, lead to user frustration, a critical issue
that may lead students to abandon the collaborative Socratic process in favor of unconstrained
chatbots that provide immediate solutions, thereby undermining the pedagogical goal.
However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The AI tutor’s primarily text-based
nature of the student-tutor interaction limits the ability to fully support pedagogical methods
requiring dynamic visual collaboration, such as the ISLE framework [20]. Furthermore, the
system inherits the intrinsic statistical nature of the underlying AI models; this means responses,

--- PAGE 10 ---
particularly for problems without curated guidance, may occasionally contain inaccuracies,
necessitating critical evaluation by users and potential oversight from educators [11].
Addressing these challenges points to clear directions for future research. Our initial
implementation of an adaptive scaffolding rule in the “Training Manual”—prompting the tutor
to be more proactive when a student struggles—points to a particularly valuable path: exploring
more dynamic strategies where the tutor adjusts its level of guidance in real-time. Ultimately,
formal user studies are required to rigorously assess both student learning and engagement
with this tutor model. As platforms like NotebookLM continue to evolve, so too will the
opportunities to enhance these capabilities. Nevertheless, the proof-of-concept detailed here
offers a promising and replicable model for educators seeking to create grounded, collaborative
AI learning assistants in physics education.
